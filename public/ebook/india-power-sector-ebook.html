1 Executive Summary & Industry Overview

India operates the world's 3rd largest electricity system — behind only China and the United States. With 450.29 GW of installed capacity serving 1.4 billion people across 36 states and union territories, the power sector is the backbone of India's $3.7 trillion economy.

The sector has undergone a remarkable 22.5x transformation — from just 20 GW at independence's silver jubilee (1975) to over 450 GW today. More significantly, the energy transition is now in full swing: non-fossil sources have grown from 24% (2000) to 45.8% (2025), and are on track to surpass fossil fuel capacity by 2028.

Solar Energy — The Growth Engine

Solar has grown from virtually zero in 2010 to 90.6 GW by March 2025 — the fastest capacity addition in India's energy history. Key facts:

  • Tariff decline: From ₹17/kWh (2010) to ₹1.99/kWh (2024) — an 88% reduction
  • Sub-segments: Utility-scale ground mount (75%), Rooftop (20%), Floating (5%)
  • Technology: Mono-PERC dominant; Bifacial and TOPCon gaining share
  • Domestic manufacturing: PLI scheme driving local production (reduce China dependency)
  • Major parks: Bhadla (2,245 MW), Pavagada (2,050 MW), Kurnool (1,000 MW)
  • MNRE Jun 2026 update: Solar has crossed 162 GW (rapid additions in FY26)

Wind Energy — The Pioneer

India was an early wind adopter (1990s) and has the world's 4th largest installed wind capacity:

  • Turbine evolution: 250 kW (1990s) → 2 MW (2010s) → 3.5-5.5 MW (2025)
  • Hub heights: Increased from 50m to 120-160m — accessing stronger, steadier winds
  • Repowering opportunity: 10+ GW of old turbines (<1 MW) eligible for 3-5x capacity upgrade
  • Offshore: First 4 GW tenders issued for Gujarat coast (deeper water, higher wind)
  • Key corridors: Muppandal (TN), Kutch (GJ), Jaisalmer (RJ), Chitradurga (KA)
  • MNRE Jun 2026 update: Wind at 57.4 GW

Coal — Still the Backbone

Despite the renewable push, coal remains India's primary generation source (67% of actual electricity). Understanding coal plant risks is critical for ICICI Lombard's portfolio:

  • Fleet age: ~40% of coal plants are 25+ years old — higher failure probability
  • Super-critical technology: 50% of new plants; higher efficiency but complex maintenance
  • Key operators: NTPC (48 GW coal), Adani Power (16 GW), Tata Power (8 GW)
  • No new coal after 2030: Only plants under construction will be completed
  • Stranded asset risk: 30-40 GW may become uneconomical by 2035 (carbon pricing risk)
💡 Underwriting Note: Solar and Wind now represent 50%+ of new policy originations in power sector. However, coal still dominates the existing portfolio (higher sum insured per MW). The transition from coal to RE requires recalibrating loss assumptions — RE has higher frequency but lower severity per event compared to thermal.

Why This Matters for Insurance

The capacity vs generation gap has direct implications for Machinery Loss of Profit (MLOP) calculations:

  • Coal (PLF 64%): A 500 MW unit generates ~2,800 MU/year at ₹4/kWh = ₹1,120 Cr revenue/year. A 6-month outage = ₹560 Cr MLOP claim.
  • Solar (CUF 18%): A 500 MW solar plant generates ~790 MU/year at ₹2.5/kWh = ₹198 Cr revenue/year. Same 6-month outage = ₹99 Cr MLOP claim.
  • Wind (CUF 21.5%): If outage occurs during peak season (Jun-Sep), BI impact is 3x higher than off-season.
🎯 Underwriting Principle: Always calculate MLOP exposure based on actual generation (PLF/CUF adjusted), not nameplate capacity. A 500 MW coal plant has 5-6x more BI exposure than a 500 MW solar plant despite same "capacity."

Coal PLF Trend — Declining Utilization

Coal PLF has declined from 78% (2007) to 64% (2025). This is not because coal plants are failing — it's because renewable energy has "must-run" priority on the grid. During daytime when solar floods the grid, coal plants are forced to back down (reduce generation). This creates a structural challenge: coal assets generate less revenue but still need full insurance coverage for the physical asset.

💡 Emerging Concern: As RE penetration increases, coal plants will operate more in "cycling" mode (frequent start-stop). This increases thermal stress and boiler tube failure frequency by an estimated 15-20%. Underwriters should factor this into technical assessment.

BESS — Deep Dive (Highest Priority for 2025-2027)

Battery Energy Storage Systems store excess solar/wind energy for use after sunset. India targets 20 GW by 2030. The primary risk is thermal runaway — when one cell overheats and triggers adjacent cells in an uncontrollable chain reaction, leading to intense fires (lithium burns at 2,000°C, cannot be extinguished with water).

  • Global incidents: Arizona (2019 — 4 firefighters injured), Australia (2021 — Tesla Megapack fire burned 3 days), South Korea (23 fires in 3 years)
  • Root causes: Manufacturing defects, overcharging, cooling system failure, external fire exposure
  • Insurance product needed: Material Damage + Fire + BI + Third-party liability (toxic gas release)
  • Pricing challenge: No Indian loss data. Must rely on global benchmarks + engineering assessment
🎯 Action Item: ICICI Lombard should develop a standalone "BESS All-Risk" product by Q1 2027 — covering thermal runaway, electrical failure, performance degradation, and environmental liability. Early-mover advantage is critical as 20 GW = ₹80,000 Cr assets will need coverage.

12.2 Key Exclusions & Sub-limits to Watch

  • AOG sub-limits: Many policies have inadequate cyclone/flood sub-limits (₹5-10 Cr when actual loss can be ₹50-200 Cr). Always review.
  • Wear & tear exclusion: Boiler tube failures in old plants often disputed as W&T vs sudden/unforeseen. Clear wording needed.
  • Consequential loss: Coal quality causing clinker → tube failure — is this excluded? Policy wording critical.
  • Waiting period (BI): 30-60 days typical. For solar/wind (repair in 7-14 days), shorter WP may be appropriate.
  • Basis of settlement: Reinstatement vs indemnity — critical for technology obsolescence (old wind turbines).
